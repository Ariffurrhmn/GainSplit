## Main points
- No single rule, but teachable heuristics: McGuire (1997, Annual Review of Psychology) lists 49 in 5 categories.
- Two ways to build a question (Sandberg and Alvesson 2010, 52 articles): **gap-spotting** (most common) and **problematization**, i.e. challenging an assumption the field holds (Alvesson and Sandberg 2011; rarer, linked to more influential work). Davis (1971): "interesting" findings deny what the audience assumed.
- Three stages: generate, prune, evaluate and rank (Vandenbroucke and Pearce 2018; Duncan et al. 2023).
- Novelty is high-risk, high-reward: highly novel papers are about 3x more likely to reach the top 1% most cited over a long window but are cited less at first (Wang, Veugelers and Stephan 2017); high-impact papers are mostly conventional with a small dose of unusual combinations (Uzzi et al. 2013).

## Recurring moves (one example each)
1. Familiar core plus one unusual ingredient (Uzzi 2013; Shi and Evans 2023).
2. Flip an assumption (Davis 1971; HypoGen "bit-flip" preprint, O'Neill et al. 2025).
3. Re-test claimed progress against a tuned simple baseline (Ferrari Dacrema et al. 2019; Adhikari et al. 2019; Lipton and Steinhardt 2018).
4. Take a bundled method apart (Lipton and Steinhardt 2018, "sources of empirical gains").
5. Find the shortcut in the data with a crippled model (Gururangan et al. 2018; Gardner et al. 2021; LLM-written data, Proebsting and Poliak 2025 preprint).
6. Pit rival explanations in one experiment, "strong inference" (Platt 1964 via Mackenzie 2018; critique O'Donohue and Buchanan 2001).
7. Follow a surprise (McGuire 1997; Garbuio and Lin 2021; Ben-David 2024).
8. Change the setting, not the method (gap-spotting, "incomplete" type).
